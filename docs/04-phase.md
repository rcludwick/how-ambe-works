# Why vocoders discard phase

<!-- Owner: part-i-mathematics agent -->

Every sinusoid in an audio signal needs three numbers to describe it.
Frequency sets the pitch, amplitude sets the volume, and [phase](18-glossary.md#phase)
marks where the wave sits in its cycle at time zero.
{: .lede }

If you change the frequency of a harmonic, the pitch moves. If you change its
amplitude, the loudness changes. But if you shift the starting phase of a harmonic
relative to its neighbors inside a steady vowel, the acoustic waveform changes
shape completely while your ear hears almost no difference at all.

This perceptual quirk is the reason low-bitrate vocoders can exist. By discarding
phase measurements at the transmitter and regenerating synthetic phase at the
receiver, the encoder saves dozens of bits in every 20-millisecond frame.

## How the ear hears harmonic phase

In 1843, Georg Ohm suggested that the human ear acts as a frequency analyzer,[^ohm]
breaking incoming sound into individual sinusoidal components. Hermann von Helmholtz
later expanded on this observation. The cochlea in your inner ear contains thousands
of sensory hair cells along the basilar membrane. Each hair cell vibrates at its own
resonant frequency.

When a periodic sound enters the ear, the hair cells report how much energy sits
at each frequency band. Because each frequency stimulates a different physical
location along the membrane, the ear measures the magnitude spectrum directly.
The relative timing between harmonic peaks across separate auditory filters is
largely lost in neural transmission.

You can verify this directly with an audio synthesizer. Take a musical note built
from ten harmonics where every harmonic starts at zero degrees phase. The resulting
waveform looks like a sharp pulse train, with high peak pressure and long valleys
of silence. Now assign a random starting phase to each of the ten harmonics.
The new waveform flattens out, looking like a band of noise. Yet when you play
both sounds through headphones, they sound almost identical.

To a speech codec, this observation is valuable. Measuring and transmitting the exact
starting phase of forty harmonics would require hundreds of bits per frame.
A listener cannot tell the difference between zero phase, random phase,
or quadratic phase inside a stationary 20-millisecond vowel. So the encoder does
not need to send phase bits over the air.

In [**Figure 1.1**](01-why-sinusoids.md#fig-1-1), you can drag the phase slider of a
sinusoid to see how phase shifts the waveform in time without changing its height or its
pitch.

## Why the seventy-two bit frame has no room for phase

The Griffin and Lim 1988 Multi-Band Excitation research coder initially attempted
to transmit phase for voiced harmonics <span class="cite">Griffin &amp; Lim 1988, §II</span>.
That required high bitrates, typically 8,000 to 9,600 bits per second.

In commercial AMBE as used in amateur radio systems like D-STAR, every 20-millisecond
speech block must compress into exactly 72 bits. That corresponds to 3,600 bits per
second total. The radio splits those bits into 48 bits for speech parameters and
24 bits for forward error correction.

The 48 voice bits in a frame leave no room for phase.

- Pitch takes 7 to 8 bits.
- Voicing decisions across frequency bands take several bits.
- Harmonic amplitude vector quantization consumes the entire remaining budget.

slightly to 122 Hz. If the decoder synthesizes each 20-millisecond frame as an isolated
block starting at phase zero, the waveform will jump abruptly at the boundary
between frames.

In discrete-time audio, a sharp jump between adjacent samples creates a step
discontinuity. The listener does not hear smooth speech. They hear a harsh click fifty
times a second, turning human voices into a loud mechanical buzz.

To synthesize natural speech without transmitted phase, the decoder must solve two
problems.

1. Within unvoiced bands, generate random noise with randomized phase across the band.
2. Within voiced bands, compute a continuous phase trajectory θ<sub>l</sub>(t) for each
   harmonic l that matches the measured frequencies at the centers of adjacent frames
   without sudden jumps.

## Cubic phase interpolation across frame boundaries

Digital Voice Systems solved the boundary problem by fitting a smooth mathematical bridge
between consecutive frames <span class="cite">US 5,081,681</span>.

For each harmonic l, the synthesizer knows the frequency at the center of frame n
and the target frequency at the center of frame n+1.
It also knows the accumulated phase angle where the harmonic oscillator left off at the
end of the previous calculation.

The decoder fits a cubic polynomial to the phase over the transition interval,
θ<sub>l</sub>(t) = c₀ + c₁t + c₂t² + c₃t³. Its four coefficients match four boundary
conditions.

- The phase starts at the accumulated value from the previous frame.
- The derivative of phase, which is instantaneous frequency, matches the measured frequency of frame n.
- The derivative at the end of the interval matches the measured frequency of frame n+1.
- The decoder adds a whole multiple of 2π to eliminate artificial phase wrapping.

In [**Figure 11.2**](11-synthesis.md#fig-11-2), you can inspect how cubic phase
interpolation glides each harmonic oscillator smoothly across the 20-millisecond
boundary, eliminating boundary clicks completely.
